In Lagash, one of the most economically advanced city-states, these conditions culminated in a political overthrow. The local ruler concentrated near-absolute ownership of half of Lagash’s land in his own hands, systematically redistributed administrative offices to his personal retinue, and increased exactions on both dependent laborers and free commoners. Concurrently, elite-driven debt bondage among the common populace reached its peak. The ruling house was overthrown in a mass uprising, though the ruler himself was spared his life and civic rights. The popular assembly subsequently elevated a certain Ur-Nanshe to power, bestowing upon him the title of *lugal* (Sumerian for king, late 24th century BCE). This new ruler abolished the excessive levies, once again separated the temple economy from the ruler’s private estates, and annulled a series of debt contracts. Comparable reforms were occasionally implemented in other Mesopotamian city-states.

In the mid-17th century BCE, Babylonia gradually began to restore its power, and around 1630 BCE, it conquered the Euphrates principality of the Kassites and the Khanir kingdom, extending its authority to the Middle Euphrates. However, during the reign of the last king of the Hammurabi dynasty, Samsu-iluna (1625–1595 BCE), a severe crisis erupted. Khanigalbat rose up against Babylonia, and the Kassites of the Gandash and later the Hana dynasties rebelled and waged war against it. Furthermore, Gulkishar, the king of the Coastal Region, also began to fight against Babylonia, and finally, in 1595 BCE, the unexpected campaign of the Hittite king Mursili I from Anatolia brought matters to a head. Marching along the Euphrates and passing through Hana, he captured and plundered Babylon. Samsu-iluna presumably died. The Hittites did not attempt to establish a foothold in Mesopotamia but turned back with enormous plunder, including the main statues of the god Marduk and his consort Tsarpani from the temple of Marduk in Babylon. The Babylonians believed that maintaining full communication with the gods was impossible without these relics.

Soon after, in the mid-15th century BCE, Babylonia was definitively (and permanently) integrated territorially: the last king of the Primordial Lands, Eagamīl, became preoccupied with wars against Elam, and the Kassite ruler Kaštiliaš III sent his brother, Ulam-Būriaš, against the Primordial Lands; the latter defeated and killed Eagamīl, and assumed the title of “king of the Primordial Lands” as the de facto “viceroy” of his brother in that territory. With the death of Ulam-Būriaš, the lands of the Primordial Lands were fully merged with Babylonia; the inhabitants of the Primordial Lands revolted, but the new king of Babylon, Agum III (son of Kaštiliaš and nephew of Ulam-Būriaš), crushed them and destroyed the temple of Ea, the patron deity of the Primordial Lands. In the same decades, the Kassites seized the Middle Euphrates valley from Hanigalbat, king of Mitanni (Mitanni was at this time embroiled in wars with the Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose III, and the Kassites took advantage of this). Notably, after the Kassite annexation of the Primordial Lands, its inhabitants were still considered a distinct sub-ethnic group, the “people of the Primordial Lands,” distinct from the Babylonians proper – the “Akkadians” – but by the end of the 2nd millennium BCE, they no longer attempted to secede from Babylonia. Thus, the country that was once called “Babylonia” finally took shape as a unified entity under the Kassites, and the ancient peoples themselves (the Assyrians, and five hundred years later, after the fall of the Kassite dynasty) were well aware of this, continuing to refer to Babylonia by its official Kassite name, “Karduniaš.”

In the summer of 333 BCE, the Greco-Macedonian army advanced through Cilicia and crossed the mountain passes into northern Syria, where the main Persian forces under Darius III were concentrated. That autumn, the second engagement took place at Issus, where Alexander secured a decisive victory.

Learning of Alexander’s successes, the Egyptian satrap admitted him into the country without resistance. The Egyptians welcomed the king, viewing him as a liberator from the hated Persians. In Memphis, priests ceremonially placed the pharaoh’s double crown upon Alexander’s head. The Oracle of Ammon, revered across both Egypt and the Greek world, declared him the son of a god—a pronouncement that profoundly shaped his later claims to divine honors. At the mouth of the Nile, Alexander founded a new city bearing his own name, Alexandria, the first of numerous Alexandrias.

It is difficult to determine whether public property law constituted a separate section in the Twelve Tables. The distinction between *res publicae* and *res privatae* was undoubtedly already recognized, yet the separation between public and private property remained relatively limited. For land, it extended only to a private allotment of two *iugera* (0.5 hectares). This conclusion is further supported by the fact that the core institutions of property law applied to both public and private holdings. For example, the *ius spei* (“right of the spear”), enforced through the *vindicatio* action, served not only to protect an individual’s claim to property but also to vindicate—that is, confiscate—property belonging to a private party or even another state for the benefit of the Roman people. This dual function reflects the ritual’s origins. The spear (*vindicta*), its principal symbol, derives from the right to declare war, specifically the fetial rite of “spearing” enemy territory and the public auctions *sub hasta* (“under the spear”) of the Roman people’s property captured from enemies.
