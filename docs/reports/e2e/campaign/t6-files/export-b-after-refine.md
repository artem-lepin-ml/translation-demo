The Stele of Naram-Suen, erected to commemorate the victory over the Lullubi, is one of the masterpieces of Akkadian art. Late 23rd century BC. Paris, the Louvre.

The Ishtar Gate, faced with glazed brick depicting bulls and dragons, led to the temple of Marduk. 6th century BC. Berlin, Pergamon Museum. The Standard of Ur with its lapis lazuli inlay is housed elsewhere: London, the British Museum.

Egyptian Thebes, the capital of Upper Egypt, reached its zenith under the 18th Dynasty. The temple complexes of Karnak and Luxor made Thebes the largest religious center of the country, and the necropolis on the west bank of the Nile received the tombs of the New Kingdom pharaohs.

Greek Thebes, the chief city of Boeotia, rose to fame in the 4th century BC, when Epaminondas crushed the Spartans at Leuctra. The Cadmea, the citadel of Thebes, was according to legend founded by Cadmus long before the Trojan War.

Memphis, the ancient capital of Egypt at the apex of the Nile delta, was the center of the cult of the god Ptah. The White Walls of Memphis gave the city its first name. Curiously, modern Memphis in Tennessee, the birthplace of Memphis jazz and blues, is named after the Egyptian city.

The Kingdom of Khana with its capital at Terqa controlled the middle Euphrates after the fall of Mari. The rulers of Khana preserved Amorite traditions and titulature, and their archives illuminate the dark centuries of Mesopotamian history.

The lands along the Euphrates between Carchemish and Emar served as a buffer between the Hittite and Mitannian spheres of influence. Control of the Euphrates crossings decided the outcome of most Syrian campaigns of the Late Bronze Age.

Ancient Babylon under Hammurabi became the political center of all southern Mesopotamia. Today the ruins of Babylon in Iraq are partially reconstructed, and the Babylon museum at the entrance to the site displays replicas of the principal finds, including a copy of the law stele.
