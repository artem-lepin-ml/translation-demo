The Stele of Naram-Sin, erected in honor of the victory over the Lullubi, is one of the masterpieces of Akkadian art. Late 23rd century BC. Paris, Louvre.

The Ishtar Gate, faced with glazed brick featuring depictions of bulls and dragons, led to the Temple of Marduk. 6th century BC. Berlin, Pergamon Museum. The Standard of Ur, inlaid with lapis lazuli, is held elsewhere: London, British Museum.

Egyptian Thebes, the capital of Upper Egypt, reached its zenith under the Eighteenth Dynasty. The temple complexes of Karnak and Luxor turned Thebes into the country's largest religious center, and the necropolis on the west bank of the Nile received the tombs of the New Kingdom pharaohs.

The Greek city of Thebes, the principal city of Boeotia, rose to prominence in the 4th century BC when Epaminondas defeated the Spartans at Leuctra. The Cadmea, the citadel of Thebes, was according to legend founded by Cadmus long before the Trojan War.

Memphis, the ancient capital of Egypt near the head of the Nile Delta, was the center of the cult of the god Ptah. The White Walls of Memphis gave the city its first name. Curiously, modern Memphis in Tennessee, the home of Memphis jazz and blues, is named after the Egyptian city.

The Kingdom of Hana, with its capital in Terqa, controlled the middle Euphrates after the fall of Mari. The rulers of Hana preserved Amorite traditions and titulary, and their archives shed light on the dark ages of Mesopotamian history.

The lands along the Euphrates between Carchemish and Emar served as a buffer between the Hittite and Mitannian spheres of influence. Control over the crossings of the Euphrates determined the outcome of most Syrian campaigns of the Late Bronze Age.

Ancient Babylon under Hammurabi became the political center of all Southern Mesopotamia. Today, the ruins of Babylon in Iraq have been partially reconstructed, and the Babylon Museum at the entrance to the archaeological site displays replicas of the major finds, including a copy of the stele with the laws.
