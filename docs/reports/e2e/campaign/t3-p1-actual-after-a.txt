In Lagash, one of the most economically advanced city-states, matters came to a revolt: The local ruler concentrated near-absolute ownership of half of Lagash’s land in his own hands, large-scale redistribution of administrative offices to his personal retinue, and increased exactions on both dependent laborers and free commoners. Concurrently, debt bondage of commune members by the nobility reached its peak. The ruling house was overthrown in a mass uprising, though he himself was spared and retained his civic rights. The popular assembly subsequently elevated a certain Uruinimgina to power, bestowing upon him the title of *lugal* late 24th century BCE). This new ruler abolished the excessive levies, once again separated the temple economy from the ruler’s private estates, and annulled a series of debt contracts. Comparable reforms were occasionally implemented in other Mesopotamian city-states.
